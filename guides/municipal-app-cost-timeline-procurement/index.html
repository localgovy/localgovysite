Municipal app cost, timeline, and procurement checklist

Clerks and CAOs get asked for a town app in open meeting. Then the quotes come back like a capital project: a long statement of work, a setup fee, an implementation fee, sometimes a charge per resident, and a timeline measured in seasons.

This page is the published version of what a municipal mobile app can cost, what should be in it, how long a launch actually takes, and the questions council and staff should ask any vendor before they vote. It is written so a staff report or agenda PDF can cite it.

I run LocalGovy. We build all-in-one mobile apps that provide hubs of relevant information for the residents of small to mid-size municipalities. The numbers below are our published rates, not a custom quote. Other vendors will have other numbers. The checklist is for all of them.

What you get

A municipal mobile app is not a Facebook page with a logo on it. Residents should have easy access to local news, events, a Yelp-like business directory, local high school games, and a chatbot that answers questions using easy-to-understand information pulled from official town sources.

On LocalGovy’s municipal app, that means:

  • A branded iOS and Android app, customized for the municipality: name, colours, departments, real information
  • Town news and alerts
  • Events residents will actually see
  • School updates, including local high school games, in the same place as everything else
  • A categorized, searchable business directory
  • An AI assistant trained with verified town information
  • An administrative dashboard staff can run without a developer
  • Staff training
  • Ongoing maintenance

We maintain and automatically update content 24/7. Staff do not have to become full-time publishers. They still have the last say: post, edit, or delete any content at any time. You do not need an IT department. You do not need extra staff.

The app, and the site if you have one, runs on Canadian servers.

If you take the town bundle, the same content system feeds the public website. A post in the dashboard can appear in the app and on the site without a second workflow. People who never install the app still get the information in a browser.

The app's main value is convenience at scale. A website is where you send someone who already went looking. An app is where the town shows up on the device they already open all day.

Procurement checklist

Ask every vendor these questions. Not just us. Put the answers in the staff report. If a vendor cannot answer in writing, that is the answer.

Cost

  1. What is the all-in monthly (or annual) rate, in Canadian dollars, for iOS and Android?
  2. Is there a setup fee, a build fee, an implementation fee, or a per-resident fee? If yes, how much, and when is it invoiced?
  3. Do we pay anything before residents can use the product?
  4. Is the first month free? How is that invoiced?
  5. Can the rate be locked, and for how long?
  6. What happens if we pause or end the service? Is there a termination fee or a minimum term?

Product

  1. What do residents actually open on day one: news, events, school updates, a business directory, alerts, a way to ask a question against official town sources?
  2. Is this a branded app in our name and colours, loaded with our real departments, or a generic shell?
  3. Is a public website included, optional, or sold only as a bundle? Can we buy a website with no app?
  4. Where are the servers? For a Canadian municipality, is the municipal stack hosted in Canada?

Staff load

  1. Do we need an IT department, a developer, or extra communications staff to keep this current after launch?
  2. Who maintains content day to day? If the vendor maintains it, do staff still have the last say to post, edit, or delete?
  3. Is there an administrative dashboard a clerk can use without writing code? Is training included?

Process

  1. Can we see a working sample in our municipality’s name before we commit, and how long does that take?
  2. What is the typical time from a yes to a public launch?
  3. Are there contracts we cannot leave, and what does “billed annually” actually bind us to?

Those sixteen questions are vendor-agnostic. A council that asks them of two or three firms will get a cleaner comparison than a beauty contest of mockups.
